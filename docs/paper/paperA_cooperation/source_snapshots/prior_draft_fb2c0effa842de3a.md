## Abstract

Evaluating a new connected automated vehicle (CAV) controller requires predicting how surrounding human-driven vehicles respond to its actions. We present WM-S, a physics-informed traffic world model that separates executable CAV controllers from learned human-driver dynamics. An intelligent-driver-model core is combined with observation-conditioned driver settings, learned lane and merge dynamics, and a gated residual. Controller code receives predicted observations during feedback rollout, allowing evaluation without fitting a controller-specific predictor. We distinguish historical teacher-forced prediction from closed-loop controller ranking and restrict zero-shot claims to checkpoints preceding exposure to the evaluated controller's data. Existing closed-loop evidence supports a bounded controller-selection capability, while paired superiority to black-box prediction remains unresolved. A separate fixed-arm SUMO comparison finds native-relative improvement but higher delay than MAPPO for the frozen imagined-policy checkpoint. Counterfactual group experiments do not establish beneficial cooperation. These results motivate evaluating world models by intervention ranking, regret, population accounting, and horizon-dependent reliability, rather than trajectory error alone. ## I. Introduction

Sparse CAVs can influence traffic beyond their own trajectories, but an intervention that slows selected vehicles may either dissipate congestion or merely relocate delay to an entrance queue. A predictive model should therefore answer a controller-conditioned question: from the same observed traffic history, which controller produces the better fleet outcome? Learning a separate trajectory predictor for each controller does not directly address an unseen controller whose feedback actions change with the predicted traffic.

WM-S separates the known intervention mechanism from the uncertain environment. CAV commands are generated by executable controller code; the model learns human responses and evolves the surrounding traffic. This factorization permits unseen-controller evaluation without learning the controller's input-output mapping. It does not guarantee transfer: inaccurate lane decisions, unobserved vehicles, source queues, and compounding longitudinal errors can still reverse controller rankings.

Our central contribution, **C1**, is an empirical study of this factorization under controller exclusion, with explicit exposure history and separate teacher-forced and closed-loop tests. We pair it with a control-oriented evaluation protocol: trajectory fidelity, controller-effect signs and magnitudes, decision regret, safety response, and complete-population accounting are assessed separately. The resulting evidence establishes a limited prediction capability; it does not certify arbitrary interventions or universal superiority to learned black-box models.

**Conditional extension A** asks whether a per-CAV policy learned in WM-S can beat MAPPO and exhibit positive causal group interaction. **Conditional backup 1** instead asks whether a WM-trained cooperation residual improves frozen MAPPO. Neither extension is asserted as a completed contribution. The frozen policy's negative comparator result remains part of the evidence whichever framing is selected. <!-- Sources: docs/ASTRA_ADVICE_20261028_2.md:7-15,27-33; docs/paper_results/E3FINAL_A.md. -->

## III. Problem Setting

Let \(x_t\) denote road traffic and source-queue state, \(\mathcal C_t\) the CAV set, and \(h_t^{\rm obs}\) the available observation history. A controller \(\pi\) maps permitted observations to CAV longitudinal and lane commands. The world model estimates controller-conditioned futures \(\hat x_{t:t+H}^{\pi}\); ground-truth simulator state is reserved for offline evaluation and explicitly labeled diagnostic initialization.

Each CAV reports its own kinematics and local neighbors within **100 m**, with timestamps, receipt age, missingness, and coverage. The design also permits an RSU sensor field covering the ramp and merge, approximately **800 m upstream and 150 m downstream**. Available channels must be bound per arm; local-policy evidence does not establish the effectiveness of a richer RSU-assisted deployment. True penetration, hidden driver types, global simulator metrics, and future trajectories are not online inputs. <!-- Sources: AGENTS.md:49-52; docs/WM_C4_DESIGN.md:24-34,82-102. -->

Experiments use the preserved SUMO simulator \cite{lopez_sumo} and highway-merge network, `assets/research/highway_merge_shepherd_long/osm.net.xml.gz`, with upstream mainline, ramp, merge, and downstream regions. [[NETWORK: verify simulator version, network hash, road lengths, lane geometry, speed limits, ramp arrival process, and driver-population specification __]]. Control decisions occur every **1 s**, with **0.5 s** physics steps; the direct-acceleration design range is **[−3, 1.5] m/s²**. Main fixed-arm penetration levels are **5/10/20/40%**; these are experimental strata, not sensed true penetration. <!-- Sources: AGENTS.md:23-26; docs/WM_C4_DESIGN.md:105-107; docs/paper_results/E3FINAL_A.md:3-12,37-74. -->

For horizon \(H\), define \(J_H\) as authoritative source waiting plus whole-road speed-deficit delay for all requested vehicles, including later entrants. Cost difference is \(\Delta J_H(\pi,r)=J_H(\pi)-J_H(r)\); negative favors \(\pi\). Saved delay is its sign reversal. Throughput, ramp/mainline service, worst-group delay, braking, time-to-collision exposure, collisions, and teleports are reported alongside cost. Initial-vehicle proxy costs are not interchangeable with this fleet-plus-source estimand. <!-- Sources: docs/paper_results/E3FINAL_A.md:7-14,76-116; docs/ASTRA_INSIGHT_20261028.md:31-37. -->

## IV. WM-S Model

### A. Controller/environment factorization

Write the transition schematically as

\[
u_{i,t}=\pi_i(o_{i,t}(\hat x_t)),\quad i\in\mathcal C_t,
\qquad
\hat x_{t+\Delta t}=F_\theta(\hat x_t,u_t,\xi_t;h_t^{\rm obs}),
\]

where \(\xi_t\) contains exogenous requests and model stochasticity. The CAV code runs on predicted observations in a **closed-loop** evaluation. In a **teacher-forced** evaluation, recorded CAV motion is supplied instead. These protocols test different capabilities. Executing the same controller code also does not establish plant equivalence: requested, issued, and realized accelerations and lane actions require separate checks. <!-- Sources: docs/WM_STATE_PLAN.md:7-17; docs/WM_C4_DESIGN.md:153-157; docs/paper_results/E5_COOPOFF_C1.md:17-19. -->

### B. IDM core and driver settings

For a human-driven vehicle, the longitudinal transition combines an IDM acceleration with a learned correction:

\[
\hat\eta_i=E_\theta(h_{i,t}^{\rm obs}),\qquad
\hat a_{i,t}=a_{\rm IDM}(\hat x_t;\eta_i)
             +g_a(k)\,r_{\theta,i}(\hat x_t,h_{i,t}^{\rm obs}),
\]

where \(k\) is elapsed rollout time since observation and \(\eta_i=\hat\eta_i\) in the learned-settings variant (population defaults in historical residual-only variants). The driver-setting vector includes desired speed, time headway, minimum gap, acceleration, and comfortable braking; neighbor/history encodings condition the learned components. Hidden simulator type labels are excluded from model inputs and losses. <!-- Sources: docs/WM_STATE_PLAN.md:49-65; docs/WM_CHANGE_LOG.md:122-130. -->

This equation describes a **versioned family**, not a retrospective description of every checkpoint. Historical residual-mode models used population settings in the physics even when an encoder emitted settings. Later hybrid models connected inferred settings to the IDM transition. Thus historical C1 success cannot be attributed to learned-settings identification; nor does predictive benefit from settings establish recovery of true driver types. [[WM-BINDING: checkpoint/config hashes; settings-to-physics path; history channels; loss weights and selected curriculum __]]. <!-- Source: docs/WM_CHANGE_LOG.md:122-130. -->

### C. Learned lanes, merge, and ramp dynamics

The historical C1 architecture combines MOBIL and mandatory-merge structure with learned corrections. The later control design instead uses a learned discretionary lane rule inside a physics safety envelope, retaining a forced ramp-merge proposal. Its merge component models first-passage acceptance, a secure-gap veto, and cooperative yielding. Ramp arrivals, source queues, entries, and exits must remain consistent with the population whose delay is scored. [[WM-LANES: final checkpoint's lane/merge/ramp parameterization, event coupling, masks, and observable entrant inputs __]]. Future recorded lanes and true driver settings are oracle diagnostics, unavailable at deployment. <!-- Sources: docs/WM_C4_DESIGN.md:162-174; docs/C4_PAPER_CONTRIBUTIONS_DRAFT.md:6-10; docs/WM_CHANGE_LOG.md:123,129-130. -->

### D. Gated residual and training

The residual accounts for mismatch left by the physics prior. Later variants use separate bounded lane corrections and saturating gates to limit late-rollout amplification. Multi-step trajectory supervision trains human dynamics; losses, gradient handling, and population support are checkpoint-specific. Ablations must distinguish population-default settings, physics-only prediction, learned settings, lane mechanisms, and residual gating. The existing change log documents bundled recipe changes and unsuccessful long-horizon fixes, so their effects cannot be assigned to individual components without an isolating comparison. <!-- Source: docs/WM_CHANGE_LOG.md:93-130. -->

## V. Policy Learning in Imagination — Conditional Slot

### A. Main extension A

A shared per-CAV actor generates acceleration and safety-masked lane decisions from deployment-observable inputs. Matched imagined branches provide saved delay \(D_S=J_B-J_S\), where \(B\) is native driving and only the CAV subset \(S\) is controlled. Define

\[
C_G=D_G-\sum_{i\in G}D_{\{i\}},\qquad
M_i=D_G-D_{G\setminus\{i\}}.
\]

Positive \(C_G\) denotes a beneficial interaction beyond summed singleton effects. A candidate training objective combines fleet, group, singleton, interaction, and marginal-in-team gains; singleton credit constrains incentives to worsen solo behavior. This signal is a proposed training mechanism, not evidence that beneficial cooperation emerged. <!-- Source: docs/WM_C4_DESIGN.md:162-174. -->

The frozen evaluated policy used a native-residual pathway; it must not be described as the completed full-actor successor. Periodic simulator collection and WM refresh are intended to reduce model bias. The original design proposed critic bootstrapping, but the current advice withdraws stale long-run-critic claims. [[A-METHOD: actual actor trainability, gradient path, objective coefficients, qualified horizon, real-credit/critic status, updates, training seeds, source hashes __]]. ## VIII. Limitations

Results are simulator-based, with structural similarity between IDM traffic and the model prior. Unseen-controller transfer is distinct from unseen-world transfer, and models exposed to evaluation-controller data lose zero-shot eligibility. Historical teacher forcing, restricted histories, baseline training differences, and incomplete paired comparisons limit C1 conclusions. Observation-conditioned methods are not validated as full sparse-RSU deployment merely by full-state diagnostic rollout.

Long-horizon lane, queue, arrival, and congestion timing errors remain important. Entrant control differs across scoring and actor paths; a defect in one path is not evidence that all actor imagination omitted entrants. Sampled-driver futures do not establish calibrated epistemic confidence. Single-checkpoint efficacy and ablations do not estimate training-seed variability. Cooperation uncertainty is large, secondary intervals are unadjusted, and observed incident zeros are not risk bounds. WM-S factors executable CAV controllers from learned human-driver dynamics to study prediction under unseen controllers.
